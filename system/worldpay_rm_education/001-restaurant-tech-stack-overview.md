# Course 001 - Genius Platform and Restaurant Tech Stack Overview

Audience: Worldpay relationship managers  
Assumption: The audience understands payments, but may know little about restaurant operations, POS, kitchen systems, digital ordering, hardware, loyalty, or enterprise restaurant technology decisions.

## Session Purpose

This first session gives Worldpay RMs a practical map of the restaurant technology stack and shows where Genius fits.

The goal is not to turn RMs into solution engineers. The goal is to help them recognize when a payments conversation is really a platform conversation.

By the end of this session, an RM should be able to:

- explain Genius as a restaurant operating platform, not just a POS
- name the major pieces of a restaurant technology stack
- understand how restaurant workflows connect to payments
- recognize fragmented-stack opportunity signals
- describe the competitor landscape at a useful, non-technical level
- know when to bring Genius into an account conversation

## Core Message

Genius is the restaurant operating platform Global Payments brings into the market.

It is not simply a register. It is a platform that can touch:

- order entry
- payments
- front-counter hardware
- customer-facing display and payment acceptance
- kitchen routing and KDS
- self-ordering
- handheld and line-busting workflows
- drive-thru workflows
- digital commerce integrations
- reporting
- operational visibility
- enterprise rollout and support

For a Worldpay RM, the key insight is this:

Payments are often the doorway, but restaurant operators make buying decisions around operational outcomes.

They care about:

- speed of service
- uptime
- lower labor friction
- fewer vendor handoffs
- consistent guest experience
- better reporting
- faster rollout
- easier support
- lower operational risk
- revenue continuity when something breaks

## Why This Matters Now

Restaurant operators are under pressure.

The Global Payments / QSR restaurant resilience research reinforces the operating reality:

- POS downtime is a meaningful daily risk for operators.
- Operators are not fully confident they can continue operating if the network is compromised.
- Labor disruption, food cost, inventory pressure, and system reliability are board-level concerns.
- Operators are investing in workforce technology, payments, AI, cloud infrastructure, security, network redundancy, and edge/local processing.

That means the platform conversation should not sound like:

"Would you like to replace your POS?"

It should sound like:

"Where does your current stack create operational risk, support burden, payment fragmentation, or visibility gaps?"

## The Restaurant Stack in Plain English

A restaurant technology stack is the connected system that helps a restaurant take orders, accept payment, route food production, serve guests, manage employees, report performance, and keep money moving.

In payments language, it is the system that decides:

- what was ordered
- where it was ordered
- how it was routed
- how it was paid for
- whether the payment matched the order
- whether the kitchen fulfilled it correctly
- whether the guest can be identified later
- whether the operator can reconcile it cleanly
- whether the store can keep operating during disruption

The POS is usually the center of gravity, but the platform is bigger than POS.

Position Genius as a unified restaurant platform.

## Genius Platform Pieces

### 1. POS / Commerce Core

What it is:

The POS is the operating center for the restaurant. It captures orders, applies menu logic, handles modifiers, connects payments, routes orders, and creates transaction data.

What Genius offers:

- restaurant POS workflows
- order capture
- menu and modifier logic
- discount and pricing support
- transaction reporting
- restaurant-specific operating flows
- enterprise deployment capability

Why RMs should care:

The POS is where payments become operations. If the POS is changing, unstable, fragmented, or difficult to support, the account may be more than a merchant-services opportunity.

Signals to listen for:

- "We are evaluating POS."
- "We are moving from Aloha to Oracle."
- "Half our stores are on one POS and half are on another."
- "Our franchisees are on different systems."
- "The POS works, but reporting is painful."
- "The POS vendor and payments provider point fingers at each other."

### 2. Payments

What it is:

Payments include card-present, card-not-present, authorization, settlement, refunds, chargebacks, fraud, reconciliation, and reporting.

What Genius / Global Payments can bring:

- integrated payment acceptance
- direct-to-processor architecture
- reduced handoffs between POS and payments
- better ownership of the transaction path
- support for restaurant-specific transaction environments

Why RMs should care:

Payments are not isolated. A restaurant transaction starts as an order and ends as money movement. If payment data does not align with POS, online ordering, loyalty, or reporting, the operator feels that pain.

Signals to listen for:

- "In-store and online payments are split."
- "OloPay is used online, but another processor handles in-store."
- "Chargebacks are becoming an issue."
- "Refunds and reconciliation take too long."
- "We cannot see payment performance by location."
- "Payments are bundled with a POS we may want to change."

### 3. Front-Counter Hardware

What it is:

The front-counter terminal is the visible restaurant workstation where orders are entered and payments are accepted.

Hardware examples to understand:

- fixed POS terminals
- customer-facing displays
- integrated payment devices
- low-profile counter mounts
- receipt printers
- cash drawers, when cash is accepted
- scanners or peripherals, depending on concept

Genius example:

For IKEA's food truck use case, the recommended bundle was a Genius GC26 front-counter POS terminal with customer-facing display and integrated payments. That type of hardware conversation matters because operators need something durable, restaurant-ready, and suited to semi-outdoor or high-traffic use.

Why RMs should care:

Hardware is often where the operator's confidence becomes real or collapses. A beautiful software demo does not matter if the counter hardware is awkward, fragile, slow, or hard to support.

Signals to listen for:

- "We need a smaller footprint."
- "We need something rugged."
- "We have semi-outdoor locations."
- "We want integrated payments at the register."
- "We need customer-facing payment."
- "We need a standard install kit for rollout."

### 4. Handhelds / Line Busting

What it is:

Handhelds let staff take orders and payments away from the fixed register. In QSR and fast casual, they can be used for line busting, patio ordering, events, drive-thru overflow, or high-volume rush periods.

Genius example:

An optional line-busting solution can include Genius all-in-one handhelds with Moby 5500-style payment acceptance.

Why RMs should care:

Handhelds are not just hardware. They are throughput tools. They help operators serve more guests during peak periods without adding a full counter station.

Signals to listen for:

- "Lines build up during lunch."
- "Drive-thru backs up."
- "We lose orders during peak periods."
- "We need mobile ordering inside the restaurant."
- "We need an event or food-truck workflow."

### 5. KDS / Kitchen Management

What it is:

A kitchen display system routes orders from POS, online ordering, kiosk, drive-thru, or handhelds to the right kitchen station.

What it controls:

- make-line routing
- prep timing
- expo workflow
- pickup workflow
- kitchen status
- order sequencing

What Genius can support:

- kitchen management
- KDS workflows
- routing from ordering channels into production
- operational visibility from order to fulfillment

Why RMs should care:

KDS is where restaurant demand turns into operational pressure. If the kitchen cannot absorb digital, drive-thru, or in-store demand, the operator's revenue opportunity becomes execution risk.

Signals to listen for:

- "Online orders overwhelm the kitchen."
- "Tickets are not routed correctly."
- "Drive-thru and counter orders collide."
- "Ticket times are inconsistent."
- "We need a KDS for a new format."
- "The kitchen does not trust the order flow."

### 6. Kiosks and Self-Ordering

What it is:

Kiosks let guests order without a cashier. They can improve throughput, reduce labor pressure, increase check size, and standardize upsell prompts.

Common market examples:

- native POS kiosks
- Bite
- QikServe
- Mashgin
- Acrelec
- STRATACACHE
- Coates

Why RMs should care:

Kiosks create payments, identity, menu, routing, and support questions. They are rarely just screens.

Signals to listen for:

- "We are testing kiosks."
- "We need to reduce front-counter labor."
- "We want consistent upsell."
- "Our kiosk orders do not flow cleanly into the POS/KDS."
- "We are adding self-ordering to high-volume units."

### 7. Drive-Thru

What it is:

Drive-thru technology can include order confirmation boards, menu boards, headsets, lane timing, AI voice ordering, payment acceptance, and kitchen routing.

Why RMs should care:

Drive-thru is often the highest-stakes operational channel in QSR. Small improvements in speed, order accuracy, payment flow, and kitchen routing can have large revenue impact.

Signals to listen for:

- "Drive-thru speed is a priority."
- "We are testing AI voice."
- "We need better order confirmation."
- "Drive-thru orders do not route cleanly."
- "We want to modernize the lane."

### 8. Digital Ordering / Online Commerce

What it is:

Digital ordering includes brand website, mobile app, catering, marketplace integrations, delivery aggregators, and order injection into POS/KDS.

Common market examples:

- Olo
- Lunchbox
- Tillster
- ChowNow
- DoorDash
- Uber Eats
- Checkmate
- Deliverect

Why RMs should care:

Digital ordering often creates split payment environments. A restaurant may process in-store through one provider and digital orders through another. That creates reconciliation, fraud, guest-data, and reporting complexity.

Signals to listen for:

- "We use OloPay online."
- "Online orders use Stripe, but stores use another processor."
- "Menu sync is hard."
- "Third-party tablets are messy."
- "Delivery orders disrupt kitchen flow."
- "Fraud or chargebacks are increasing online."

### 9. Loyalty / CRM / Guest Engagement

What it is:

Loyalty and CRM tools identify guests, manage rewards, send offers, and drive repeat visits.

Common market examples:

- Punchh
- Paytronix
- Thanx
- Olo Engage
- SessionM
- Incentivio
- Toast Loyalty

Why RMs should care:

Loyalty needs transaction data. Payments can become strategically relevant when the operator wants to connect spend, guest identity, offers, app activity, and frequency.

Signals to listen for:

- "We are rebuilding the app."
- "We want better guest data."
- "We cannot connect loyalty to payment behavior."
- "We need more repeat visits."
- "Corporate owns loyalty, but franchisees own execution."

### 10. Back Office, Reporting, Inventory, and Labor

What it is:

Back-office systems handle accounting, inventory, invoices, food cost, labor cost, scheduling, forecasting, training, and manager workflows.

Common examples:

- Restaurant365
- Crunchtime
- MarginEdge
- MarketMan
- xtraCHEF
- HotSchedules / Fourth
- Harri
- 7shifts
- Legion

Why RMs should care:

These systems expose how fragmented the operator's data really is. The RM may not sell these tools directly, but the symptoms often point back to POS, payments, reporting, and integration gaps.

Signals to listen for:

- "Food cost is hard to control."
- "Labor scheduling is painful."
- "We cannot see profitability by store."
- "Reconciliation is manual."
- "We are acquiring locations and need a common view."

### 11. Data, AI, Edge, and Resilience

What it is:

This is the layer that determines whether the restaurant can keep operating, learn from its data, and adopt new automation.

Important concepts:

- cloud vs local processing
- offline order capture
- network redundancy
- edge computing
- data visibility
- AI readiness
- reporting consistency

Why RMs should care:

Enterprise buyers increasingly ask platform questions through the language of resilience, AI, and operating visibility.

The resilient restaurant research points to this shift: operators want faster transaction processing, better system connectivity, real-time analytics, reliability, personalization, and business continuity.

Signals to listen for:

- "Can stores keep taking orders if internet goes down?"
- "What happens if payments are unavailable?"
- "We need better reporting."
- "We are looking at AI."
- "We need local resilience."
- "We want fewer vendors."

## How the Stack Connects

Here is the simplest flow:

1. Guest places order.
2. POS captures the order.
3. Payment is authorized.
4. Order routes to kitchen/KDS.
5. Kitchen fulfills the order.
6. Guest receives food.
7. Transaction data flows into reporting, loyalty, accounting, and analytics.
8. Operator uses the data to manage performance.

When this flow works, the restaurant feels fast and controlled.

When it breaks, the operator experiences:

- long lines
- kitchen confusion
- payment failures
- refund issues
- bad reporting
- manual reconciliation
- guest complaints
- franchisee frustration
- support escalation

Examples:

Typical challenges to discover:

### 3. Unified Platform

Example:

Typical strengths to position:

- system-wide economics
- brand consistency
- top-line growth
- vendor consolidation
- data visibility
- AI roadmap
- lower franchisee noise
- rollout risk

"Where is the current estate creating cost, complexity, or lack of visibility at the brand level?"

### Franchisee

Franchisees care about:

- store-level profitability
- speed
- reliability
- support
- training time
- low disruption

### Restaurant Manager

Managers care about:

## Discovery Questions RMs Can Use

Start broad:

- What POS are you using today?
- Is that consistent across corporate and franchise locations?
- Are you evaluating any POS or gateway changes?
- Who owns POS decisions: IT, operations, finance, franchisees, or procurement?
- Who owns payments decisions?
- Are in-store and online payments processed the same way?
- What breaks during peak hours?
- Where does support get messy?
- What systems create the most manual reconciliation?
- Are you testing kiosks, handhelds, drive-thru changes, AI, or kitchen modernization?

Go deeper:

- What happens if the internet goes down?
- Can stores keep taking orders?
- Can stores keep taking payments?
- Does the kitchen receive every order source in one workflow?
- How many vendors touch a single guest transaction?
- Where do franchisees complain most?
- What are you trying to standardize this year?
- What new formats are you testing?
- Where do you need better visibility by location?

## The RM's Translation Job

When a merchant says:

"We are looking at POS."

Translate to:

"There may be a platform window. We need timing, decision makers, current stack, payments ownership, and operational pain."

When a merchant says:

"We use OloPay online but another processor in-store."

Translate to:

"There may be payment fragmentation, reconciliation pain, and data inconsistency."

When a merchant says:

"We are moving to Oracle."

Translate to:

"Do not assume the deal is over. Understand whether the migration is complete, what pain drove the decision, what remains unresolved, and whether adjacent wedges exist."

When a merchant says:

"We are piloting kiosks."

Translate to:

"This touches menu, payments, POS routing, KDS, guest experience, support, and rollout economics."

When a merchant says:

"Stores are frustrated with support."

Translate to:

"This may be an ownership-gap problem. Genius should explore whether unified platform/support accountability matters."

## What Not To Do

Do not:

- lead with SKU names
- assume every account is a POS replacement
- attack the incumbent vendor
- overclaim competitor weaknesses
- turn the competitor matrix into a fight
- pitch hardware without understanding workflow
- pitch software without understanding operations
- separate payments from the rest of the restaurant transaction
- leave without identifying next step and owner

Do:

- ask clean stack-mapping questions
- listen for operational pressure
- identify decision ownership
- verify timing
- bring Genius in when the account has platform pain
- document the current stack
- capture the wedge
- create the next action
