## Commercial and rollout test

Separate upfront hardware, kiosk software, KDS, DMB, installation, survey/staging, managed support, payment integration, and maintenance by store and by payer. Compare new costs with displaced costs and identify the corporate/franchisee share. At QSR's estimated 2025 U.S. average unit sales of $490,000, a $100 monthly store fee equals $1,200 annually, roughly 0.245% of sales; this is a sensitivity illustration, not an operating-margin estimate. Show low-volume and multi-unit cases, financing options only if approved and priced, and a credible survey/installation model that does not presume every operator will buy a premium professional install. Pilot, production, and 2027 capacity should be checked against labor, subcontractors, inventory, freight, site readiness, and support staffing. Sources: Subway 2024 U.S. FDD; QSR 50 2026; RFP Enclosures 1–3.

## Strategic discussions beyond the requested scope

The compliant RFP answer should stand on its own. Add a short, clearly labeled invitation in the executive summary or cover letter to explore two adjacent opportunities after the formal bid process permits discussion; do not put an unrequested POS substitution or unpriced payments subsidy in the requirement grids.

First, test whether Global Payments and Worldpay can help Subway lower the store-level cash burden through a funded SaaS model and, especially, hardware-as-a-service for kiosks, KDS, DMB, or future POS devices. This is an option to model, not a promise that processing revenue can fund equipment. The commercial team must establish the current payment relationship and contract rights, who pays at corporate versus franchisee level, payment volume and unit economics, underwriting, term, ownership, residual value, service and replacement obligations, early termination, and whether a low-cost install path is feasible. Present a priced comparison of upfront purchase, monthly HaaS, and any payments-linked option only after those facts are validated. The 2024 FDD's HP HaaS package indicates that Subway has used this structure historically, but its current terms and operator uptake need confirmation.

Second, offer a separate discovery exercise on SubwayPOS total cost of ownership and a possible Genius POS alternative. Subway currently specifies its proprietary Windows POS and Windows 11 IoT LTSC terminals; fulfill that baseline in this RFP. Any future comparison should include internal development and QA, integrations, security and compliance, Windows licenses, device acquisition and replacement, deployment, support, upgrades, and the cost of change across franchisees. Compare those costs with Genius software subscription, Android terminal economics, integration and migration costs, required Subway-specific features, data/control rights, and operational outcomes. Do not claim Genius is cheaper until a like-for-like model and product fit are proven. Position this as a voluntary roadmap conversation, with Subway retaining the choice of when or whether to evaluate a POS change.

Suggested customer-facing bridge: “Our response addresses the technology and rollout requirements in this RFP. As a longer-term partner, we would also welcome the opportunity, at the appropriate stage, to work with Subway on two related economic questions: whether alternative funding and hardware-as-a-service structures can reduce the upfront burden on franchisees, and how the full cost of maintaining SubwayPOS compares with a modern managed POS option. We would assess both against Subway's operating requirements and franchisee economics before recommending any change.”
