## Macro Economics and System Structure

The U.S. and international stories differ. The Wisconsin regulator lists Subway's 2026 franchise filing as effective April 30, 2026. Published transcriptions of its Item 20 show U.S. franchised outlets falling from 20,576 at the start of 2023 to 18,773 at the end of 2025, with net losses of 443, 631, and 729 stores in those years. That is an 8.8% decline over three years, not a growth estate for a blanket U.S. rollout assumption. The raw 2026 filing should be checked before using the figures in a commercial commitment. [Wisconsin filing record](https://apps.dfi.wi.gov/apps/FranchiseSearch/details.aspx?hash=1703189294&id=641453&search=external&type=GENERAL); [Item 20 transcription](https://fddindex.com/).

Restaurant Business, reporting from the 2026 FDD, says Subway's franchisor earned about $689 million in 2025 net income on $925 million in revenue, while royalty revenue fell about 10% across two years. Those are franchisor results, not store profits. Corporate cash generation can coexist with shrinking domestic unit count and pressured operators. [Restaurant Business FDD analysis](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/financing/subways-corporate-profit-has-taken-under-roark-capital).

Restaurant Dive reported that total franchise revenue fell from $818 million in 2024 to $767 million in 2025, a decline of more than 6%. QSR estimates 2025 U.S. system sales at $9.2 billion, compared with its published 2024 figure of $9.653 billion, about a 4.7% decline. The two publications use different definitions and sources, but both point to a weaker domestic revenue base rather than merely fewer low-volume stores. A 2026 bankruptcy filing by a multi-unit Subway operator, MTF Subs, is an additional stress example—not evidence that all franchisees are insolvent. [Restaurant Dive FDD reporting](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/subway-closes-3k-units-since-2021/819216/); [QSR 50 2026](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/story/qsr-50-2026-top-fast-food-sandwich-chains-ranked-by-sales/); [QSR 50 2025](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/QSR_QSR50_AUGUST2025.pdf); [Bangor Daily News on MTF Subs](https://www.bangordailynews.com/2026/03/12/hancock/mtf-subs-bankruptcy-maine/).

Internationally, Subway says it has more than 35,000 restaurants worldwide, independently run by thousands of franchisees. Its December 2025 Qatar announcement said more than 25 master-franchise agreements signed in four years carried over 10,000 future restaurant commitments. Commitments are not opened stores or near-term equipment orders. The change toward larger master franchisees means buying power and technology control may sit with a regional operator rather than an individual store. [Subway company profile](https://newsroom.subway.com/About-Subway); [Subway Qatar announcement](https://newsroom.subway.com/Press-Releases?item=122557).

## Micro Economics of a Subway Operator

Subway's 2024 disclosure document required an 8% royalty and 4.5% advertising fee, both assessed weekly on gross sales. QSR's 2026 ranking estimates Subway's 2025 U.S. system sales at $9.2 billion and average sales per unit at $490,000. At that sales level, the two franchise fees alone would be about $61,250 per year before food, labor, occupancy, utilities, debt service, repairs, or owner pay. The $490,000 is an industry estimate, not Subway's Item 19 representation, and a system average masks large store-to-store variation. QSR also estimates Jersey Mike's 2025 AUV at $1.36 million and Jimmy John's at $1.0 million; format and ownership differences prevent treating these as direct profitability comparisons, but they show Subway's low sales productivity relative to major sandwich peers. [Subway 2024 FDD, Item 6](https://storage.googleapis.com/wefranch-files/media/098057a4-577a-42d1-83c4-41ec959541a2/fdd_orig-636253_20240624.pdf); [QSR 50 2026 sandwich ranking](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/story/qsr-50-2026-top-fast-food-sandwich-chains-ranked-by-sales/).

The 2024 FDD also listed a roughly $75 monthly restaurant-technology fee and an HP hardware-as-a-service base package of roughly $57 monthly, excluding tax and shipping. That is about $1,584 a year together before payment terminals or other technology. Digital menu boards could add $8,000–$14,000 installed as a purchase or about $155 a month under a hardware-as-a-service option; these are 2024 terms that need current verification. The document says SubwayPOS software was developed by Subway affiliates and HP was then the approved POS hardware vendor. This **confirms HP's later role but does not establish that HP beat PAR in 2000–01**. [Subway 2024 FDD, Items 5–6 and 11](https://storage.googleapis.com/wefranch-files/media/098057a4-577a-42d1-83c4-41ec959541a2/fdd_orig-636253_20240624.pdf).

The same FDD made no Item 19 financial-performance representation for a franchisee. Consequently, public information does not establish a typical store operating margin, free cash flow, or capacity to absorb a new RFP fee. My recollection that some operators used informal labor instead of paid professional register installation is a historical account story, not a verified systemwide practice today. It is a warning to design a low-friction, low-cost deployment option with clear quality controls—not a reason to assume operators will accept unsafe or unsupported installation. [Subway 2024 FDD, Item 19](https://storage.googleapis.com/wefranch-files/media/098057a4-577a-42d1-83c4-41ec959541a2/fdd_orig-636253_20240624.pdf).

The operator's investment decision also depends on what headquarters mandates and who captures the benefit. A new capability that helps corporate reporting but requires franchisee hardware, downtime, or ongoing fees may face resistance even when the franchisor can fund it. Conversely, a centrally funded rollout with lower store charges may be commercially feasible for the system even if store cash flow is tight. The RFP model must identify the payer, the beneficiary, the existing fee it replaces, and the rollout cohort.

### Illustrative fee sensitivity, not a claim about actual margins

At $490,000 annual sales, every additional $100 per month is $1,200 a year, or 0.245 percentage points of sales. If a store had $14,700 in pre-tax operating profit—a **hypothetical** 3% margin—that fee would consume 8.2% of profit. A $155 monthly charge would consume 12.7%; $287 monthly for the 2024 FDD's $75 technology fee, $57 HP base package, and $155 digital-menu-board option would total $3,444 annually, or 23.4% of that hypothetical profit. These existing fees should not all be attributed to a new Global Payments bid. The exercise shows why an RFP model must distinguish a genuinely incremental cost from a displaced incumbent cost or a demonstrable operating benefit.

| Illustrative U.S. store sales | 12.5% royalty + advertising | $100 monthly new fee as share of sales | $100 monthly fee as share of hypothetical 3% profit |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $350,000 | $43,750 | 0.34% | 11.4% |
| $490,000 | $61,250 | 0.24% | 8.2% |
| $650,000 | $81,250 | 0.18% | 6.2% |

The same nominal technology fee bites harder at lower-volume stores. A proposal that is affordable at an average or top-quartile unit may be unacceptable at a $350,000 unit. Store-level sales distributions, not only the average, are needed for credible pricing.

## Technology Environment and PAR History

My 2000–01 recollection points to PAR losing a major hardware program tied to Subway's home-grown POS software. The 2014 and 2018 PAR filings show later hardware, services, and POS-related business with Subway franchisees. The 2024 Subway FDD is more precise for the U.S. franchise model at that time: Subway-affiliate SubwayPOS software and an HP-approved hardware-as-a-service program. This layered history is enough to investigate incumbent relationships, sunk costs, hardware standards, support arrangements, and switching costs; it is not enough to label PAR the current incumbent for any RFP product. Resolve current architecture by country and franchise group before making a POS displacement claim. [PAR 2014 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/708821/000114036115013699/form10k.htm); [PAR 2018 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/708821/000070882119000017/par12311810-k.htm); [Subway 2024 FDD](https://storage.googleapis.com/wefranch-files/media/098057a4-577a-42d1-83c4-41ec959541a2/fdd_orig-636253_20240624.pdf).

### Who controls the U.S. franchise technology stack

The 2024 U.S. FDD gives Subway substantial control over **standards, approved vendors, required applications, integrations, data, and upgrades**, while franchisees pay many of the resulting charges. Item 8 requires products and services from approved sources, prohibits franchisees from independently sourcing substitutes, and lets Subway change specifications and supplier designations. It listed HP as the sole approved POS hardware supplier; Subway-affiliate SubwayPOS as the sole approved POS software; Subway Payment Manager as required; Adyen as the then sole approved acquirer/processor; and a designated terminal for required gift-card, loyalty, remote-ordering, and integrated-payments programs. These are U.S. terms in 2024, not a verified statement of the September 2026 incumbent list. [Subway 2024 FDD, Item 8](https://storage.googleapis.com/wefranch-files/media/098057a4-577a-42d1-83c4-41ec959541a2/fdd_orig-636253_20240624.pdf).

Item 11 requires POS transaction and other operating data to be transmitted to Subway, including near-real-time LiveIQ reporting. The operator is contractually required to update the POS and meet future hardware standards on Subway's compliance date; Subway may require payment-terminal changes. Subway's 2024 FDD said it could approve additional vendors in the future and charge for required new hardware or software. Digital menu boards were then optional to buy or lease, although Subway reserved the right to require them later. The room for operator choice was therefore mainly within Subway's approved list and optional product programs, rather than a free choice of core POS or payment stack. [Subway 2024 FDD, Item 11](https://storage.googleapis.com/wefranch-files/media/098057a4-577a-42d1-83c4-41ec959541a2/fdd_orig-636253_20240624.pdf).

For the RFP, the likely enterprise buyer can select a standard, but actual rollout economics still land at store level unless corporate or a master franchisee funds them. Clarify whether the RFP seeks to replace an approved supplier, add a new approved option, or deploy an adjacent product; each has a different approval path and incumbent displacement risk. International master-franchise contracts may allocate control differently and are outside this U.S. FDD's scope.

## Opportunity Assessment

The RFP creates a live, multi-product evaluation according to my September 15 update. The immediate opportunity is to improve the response's product-specific fit and commercial clarity. A bundled proposal may reduce total cost or integration burden, but that is a hypothesis to model against Subway's actual scoring and franchisee purchasing rules. The existing Genius KDS relationship may provide proof of deployment capability only after the team confirms its contracted, live, and supported footprint.

## Key Discovery Questions

1. Which products and markets are in the RFP, what are the submission and decision dates, and what is the scoring weight of price versus capability, implementation, and support?
2. What exactly did PAR supply historically, to which franchise groups, and what does it supply today? Is PAR bidding in this RFP?
3. Who pays each cost: Subway corporate, a purchasing cooperative, master franchisees, or individual operators? Are prices required per store, per transaction, per region, or as an enterprise total?
4. What are the incumbent contract end dates, hardware reuse constraints, integration obligations, and migration costs?
5. Which Genius KDS locations are contracted, installed, live, and referenceable, and what operating outcome can be substantiated?
6. Who owns the RFP response, pricing approval, technical architecture, franchisee economics, and executive relationships on our side and Subway's?

## Risks and Cautions

Avoid using PAR's historical SEC disclosures as proof of current incumbency or the 2024 HP disclosure as proof of the 2000–01 vendor decision. Avoid treating a KDS selection as proof of completed deployment. The consumer value menu supports a value-oriented business context, while the statement that the brand is very price sensitive comes directly from my account update. Confirm the RFP's actual pricing terms and the payer at each layer before committing to discounts or a bundled commercial model.

## Recommended Engagement Strategy and Immediate Actions

I should join the RFP team's internal review with a product-by-product requirements matrix, a clear ownership map, and the prior Subway account record. I should ask for the actual RFP, addenda, incumbent and PAR history, installed Genius footprint, customer contacts, and current pricing model. For each proposed product, the team should show the required capability, implementation path, support model, dependencies, exclusions, and total cost to a franchisee. Any savings claim should have a traceable baseline and a named owner for validation.

## Bottom Line

The RFP is a more immediate pursuit than the governed account record currently reflects. The franchisor may have strong economics while many U.S. operators face low sales, fixed fees, remodel capital, and technology charges. The response should turn the reported price sensitivity into a transparent per-store and regional unit-economics model, and turn the PAR history into precise incumbent questions rather than an assumed competitive position.
